# 02 — Research Notes

## Purpose

To understand, from real people, what students ask, what staff struggle with, and where a wrong answer causes the most harm — before designing anything.

## Who to interview

**5 admitted / prospective students** and **2 admissions staff.**

That is 7 interviews in total. It is a small sample, and that is honest to say. Seven interviews do not prove a university-wide pattern. They do show real needs, real language, and real failure points — enough to design a first version and to know what to test next.

### How to choose participants (template)

- **Students:** Mix admitted students (who have been through the process) and prospective students (who are in it now). Include at least one student who applied from outside the local area, and at least one who asked questions in a language other than English, if that reflects your student population.
- **Staff:** One person who answers front-line enquiries (email / phone / walk-in) and one person who owns the documents — prospectus, fee circular, or calendar — so you learn both what is asked and what is hard to keep correct.

### Consent and anonymity

- Tell each person what the interview is for, that participation is voluntary, and that you will anonymise their answers.
- Do not record names, application numbers, or personal details in these notes.
- Do not quote anyone by name. If you use a quote, label it EXAMPLE unless it is a real, anonymised quote you collected with consent.

## Interview guide — template questions

Use these as a starting point. Do not read them like a script. Follow up on what the person actually says.

### For students (template)

1. What was the hardest part of applying? What did you worry about getting wrong?
2. What questions did you ask the university, and where did you ask them — email, phone, in person, website?
3. Did you ever get two different answers to the same question? What happened?
4. Which answer mattered most to you — deadline, fee, documents, hostel, scholarship? Why?
5. How did you check whether an answer was correct? Did you trust it?
6. Was there a time you could not get an answer in time? What did you do?
7. What language did you prefer to ask in? Was it easy to get help in that language?
8. If an assistant answered you, what would make you trust it? What would make you ask for a human instead?

### For admissions staff (template)

1. What questions do you answer most often in peak weeks? Which ones take the most time?
2. Where do wrong answers come from — outdated documents, memory, different sources, or something else?
3. Which wrong answer worries you most? What happens when it happens?
4. How do you check a fee or a deadline before you answer? How long does that take?
5. What questions should an assistant never answer alone?
6. What would make you trust an assistant's answer enough to let it reply without you checking first?
7. How do students ask questions in different languages? What gets lost?

## The 40 real questions — how to collect them

Collect **40 real questions** from enquiry emails, FAQs, and help-desk logs (anonymised).

Steps:

1. Ask for permission to use anonymised logs. Remove names, email addresses, application numbers, and any personal details before you store anything.
2. Copy each question exactly as the student asked it, in their own words, including spelling and language.
3. Tag each question with a topic: deadline, fee, document, hostel, scholarship, eligibility, or other.
4. Count the topics. The most frequent topics are your top 10 question types (see below).
5. Ask staff: "Which of these, if answered wrong, causes the most harm?" That gives you the 5 highest-harm questions.

## Limitations

- 7 interviews is a small sample. It can reveal needs and risks, but it cannot prove how common each need is across all applicants.
- Help-desk logs show what students asked, not what they needed but did not ask — for example, students who gave up without contacting the university.
- Language needs may be under-counted if logs are mainly in one language. Ask staff and students directly which languages students actually use.
